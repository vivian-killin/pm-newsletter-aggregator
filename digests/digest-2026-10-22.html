Three new deep dives: elastic compute, trader workflows, and the Claude Mods paradigm shift
TL;DR — PM TakeawayThis week's three pieces address compute efficiency (why agents run in rectangles), workflow fit (how AI must adapt to expert users, not the reverse), and interface design (the shift from machine-facing to human-facing agent harnesses). Core patterns for building AI products that ship.
Read more

Why AI Compute Comes in Rectangles: Erik Bernhardsson (Modal CEO) explains elastic GPU economics and why agent clouds optimize around predictable shapes. Takeaway for PMs: understand the compute footprint your product imposes; it directly affects margin and speed-to-market.

You are the Team: Anekha Sokhal (Moshi) shows how market data tools must fit the trader's workflow, not force traders to adapt. Lesson: AI features fail when they ignore domain expertise. Claude Mods: Dan McAteer frames Claude Mods as an inversion of the agent harness—shifting from machine-centric to human-centric design. This is a paradigm shift in how interface layers work with AI. PMs should study this closely.

TL;DR — PM TakeawayYang built a video-to-short converter that adds B-roll, motion graphics, captions, and music—then lets users give frame-level feedback to refine output. This is a working example of human-in-the-loop AI product design: automation that preserves editorial control.

Yang released a free AI skill (available now) that handles the tedious parts of short-form video production: adding visual elements, graphics, captions, and music to raw recordings. The key design choice is the feedback loop—users can comment on any frame, creating an iterative refinement cycle rather than a one-shot transformation.

This is a PM case study in how to layer AI with human judgment. Instead of asking users to trust an end-to-end system, Yang gives them control knobs at the point they care most: the final output. The product respects the creator's intent while automating the labor.

Rich Mironov

Jun 7, 2026
Worth your time regardless of when it was published
Why it's worth your timeMironov critiques one-shot project funding models and explains why organizations that treat software as discrete, ship-and-move-on initiatives will inevitably fail. This applies directly to AI-first product orgs funding agent or automation projects as isolated projects rather than continuous platforms.
Read more

The piece argues that funding software as one-time projects—"ship v1.0, move the team to the next thing"—creates structural failure: teams can't iterate, outcomes don't compound, and spending balloons without delivery. The cure is to fund products as ongoing platforms with persistent teams and learning cycles.

In the AI era, this becomes sharper: teams building AI agents or automation as time-limited projects will ship brittle systems that can't adapt as models improve or user needs clarify. The products that win are those with continuous investment and feedback loops baked in from day one.
