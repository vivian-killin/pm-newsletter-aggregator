Two must-reads: Elena Verna's 6-Tier AI-Nativeness Framework + Dan Shipper's 10 Bold Predictions

Article 1 (May 21): "You'll Lose Your Job in 2027" — Elena Verna via Lenny's Newsletter

Elena Verna's cross-post is the bluntest, most actionable career survival guide circulating right now. The premise: even if you keep your seat, your actual job — responsibilities, tools, scope, team size — is being rewritten every day. Her advice isn't to panic. It's to prepare now, before someone else decides what version of your job survives. "I technically stayed at the same company. But I do not have the same job."

Her action plan: first, be honest about whether you like what you do (competition gets more intense as AI levels the field, and you won't beat someone who loves the work). Second, build career optionality — start solopreneurship momentum before you need it. Third, and most actionable: become AI-native. She lays this out as a concrete 6-tier ladder:

🔢 Elena Verna's 6 Tiers of AI-Nativeness

  1. Tier 1 — Writing Buddy: Use AI to improve written communication. Pro tip: write your own version first, then have AI critique it so you actually learn. Prompts: "Make this more clear" / "Rewrite this so a skeptical exec would buy in."
  2. Tier 2 — Meeting Partner: Granola in every meeting — writes todos, tracks commitments, gives manager-level feedback. Prompts: "If you were my manager, what are my strengths and areas for improvement?" / "What did I miss that I should follow up on?"
  3. Tier 3 — Thinking Partner: LLMs will "Great point!" you to death if you let them. You have to explicitly demand disagreement. Prompts: "Disagree with me. What's the strongest counter-argument?" / "What am I missing or being naive about here?" / "Give me 20 ideas, then cut to the 3 best."
  4. Tier 4 — Builder: Internal tools, prototypes, landing pages. Prompts: "Build me a working prototype of this flow" / "Turn this PRD into something I can click through."
  5. Tier 5 — Shipping to Prod: Non-technical people pushing code to production. Instant job security upgrade. Prompts: "Fix this bug" / "Change this text" / "Make the following changes to this flow."
  6. Tier 6 — Agents: Automations running on a schedule with real oversight. Prompts: "Every Monday, summarize last week's sales and post to #revenue" / "Watch competitor pricing pages and ping me on any changes." Warning: "That AI chatbot on your site is NOT an agent."
"AI Strategy decks? Straight. To. Jail. If you don't know what matters, what good looks like, or where to focus… AI just helps you do the wrong thing faster."

Article 2 (May 24): "The AI Paradox: More Automation, More Humans, More Work" — Dan Shipper on Lenny's Podcast

Dan Shipper (CEO of Every, 30 people, 7-figure ARR) returned to Lenny's podcast with a set of contrarian takes grounded in running an actual AI-native company. Every single person at Every uses AI heavily — and yet their headcount doubled this year and SaaS spend is up. That's the paradox his episode title is named for.

His 10 bold predictions for what's actually true right now:

  1. The future of work will happen inside Codex or Claude Code — not inside your SaaS tools. Dan now does email, research, and documents inside his agent's GUI. "The CLI era is over. GUIs are back, but they live inside your agent's UI."
  2. Every company will have one "super-agent" in Slack — not one per employee. His view flip: agents need humans who care about them. When someone gets tired of maintaining their personal agent, it dies. The model that wins is one forward-deployed engineer maintaining a company-wide agent (like Shopify's River).
  3. SaaS is not dead — he's actually bullish on SaaS stocks. When users bring their own AI (via Codex/Claude Code), users pay for tokens — not the SaaS company. This improves SaaS margins. Agents also need SaaS seats, which adds demand.
  4. PMs will thrive — "Any PM who gets really AI-native will be incredibly dangerous, because the building is done for you — what matters is figuring out what to build and if it's great."
  5. Full-stack designers are becoming superheroes — they can now ship their vision without the handoff bottleneck. Huge opportunity for designer-founders.
  6. No AI job apocalypse — but you must "ride the models." Use them for everything. Try new models when they drop. "Models make yesterday's human competence cheap" — staying at the frontier is the only moat.
  7. Automation is a lie — every automation needs a human to manage it. Every doubled in size this year despite being AI-forward. "Benchmarks measure AI on problems we've already framed and can score — there's always a higher frame."
  8. Build for humans and agents to use together — current apps are built for either humans or CLIs. The future is both simultaneously. New design requirements: approval flows, inboxes that summarize what happened, logs, easy rollback.
  9. We will read more AI-generated writing and like it — for internal docs and strategy, AI writing is often better because most humans are bad at writing strategy documents.
  10. Forward deployed engineer is the new must-have role — hands-on, loves maintaining internal agents, different from traditional software engineering. "As models get better, this role evolves but doesn't disappear."
💡 PM takeaway: Elena's 6 tiers give you a concrete self-assessment tool — where are you honestly? Most PMs are at Tier 2. The Tier 3 jump (AI as thinking partner that challenges you, not validates you) is where the real leverage shift happens. Dan's framing on PMs is equally critical: your role is about to become more irreplaceable, but only if you level up to Tier 4+. Figuring out what to build matters more when building is cheap.

📌 John Cutler — AI Won't Save the Kingdoms We Built

The people who built the fiefdoms are now complaining about the fiefdoms

Cutler published his sharpest essay in months on May 23. The setup: the same leaders who built bloated ZIRP-era org structures are now promising that AI will tear them down. He doesn't buy it — and his diagnosis of why is worth sitting with.

The fiefdom model was built on plausible assumptions: autonomy creates speed, business accountability creates focus, you need leaders at the head of things as orgs scale. Dunbar's number felt like a reasonable guide. The result: smaller worlds, each with a leader, each with accountability. And in the ZIRP era, product leadership grew by stacking more "products" — often bundles of work that weren't products in any meaningful end-to-end sense.

"Anyone working in those environments felt the problem viscerally. Dependencies compounded. The end-to-end experience became harder to reason about. One team's work created a blast radius for another team. The customer did not experience the org chart as cleanly as the org chart experienced itself."

His key argument on AI as savior: "Context transfer is not the same thing as shared understanding. AI does not clarify who decides. AI does not neutralize incentive misalignment. AI does not resolve contested ownership. AI does not create the slack needed to coordinate well."

And his warning about the new pathology — "coordination theater" — is the freshest framing in this essay. People with a bias toward working alone now have a new quiver of avoidance tactics: set up agents, send copious docs, let agents talk to each other. The front lines are now drowning in async noise instead of org-chart politics. Same problem, new shape.

The real opportunity AI opens up: maybe, finally, we make the hard org choices that should have been made anyway — clearer end-to-end ownership, decision rights that match responsibility, senior leaders willing to prioritize tradeoffs. "AI can amplify a coherent operating model. It cannot substitute for one."

💡 PM takeaway: Before pitching "AI-powered cross-functional alignment" to your exec team, read this. The bottleneck in most orgs isn't information friction — it's that no one wants to own the hard tradeoffs. AI surfaces context faster, but it doesn't make the decision. Ask "who decides?" before asking "how do we share more information?"

📌 Gergely Orosz — AI's Impact on Engineers in 2026: 900+ Responses, Uncomfortable Findings

Codebase quality is declining. Management doesn't care. Yet.

Gergely published Part 2 of his 2026 survey on AI's impact on software engineers (May 20), based on 900+ responses from Pragmatic Engineer subscribers. The findings cut against the prevailing optimism:

  • Codebase quality is decreasing — and management at most companies doesn't care. Yet. This is a lagging indicator that will surface as a maintenance bill in 6-12 months.
  • AI tools reduce tedious work, but create unrealistic expectations — when engineers move faster with AI assist, business stakeholders immediately raise the bar on scope. The velocity gains get re-absorbed into scope creep rather than creating slack.
  • Team-level adoption is lagging individual adoption — companies are struggling to make AI tools feel productive at the team and org level, even where individuals are thriving.
  • Engineering culture predicts AI outcomes — the survey's most important finding: the benefits of AI tools heavily depend on the engineering culture that was in place before AI. High-trust, high-autonomy, high-feedback cultures benefit most. Low-trust cultures accelerate existing dysfunction.

His separate "Pulse" issue (May 19) also covered "forward deployed engineering heating up again" — big tech companies are hiring FDEs faster, and the role is increasingly blurring PM and engineer. He also revisited Fred Brooks' 1986 "No Silver Bullets" essay in the AI age (May 13) — finding it holds up better than expected. Complexity doesn't disappear; it shifts.

💡 PM takeaway: The quality debt signal is the one to track right now — it's not visible in velocity metrics but will surface in 6-12 months. Push your team to make quality metrics explicit before management dismisses the concern as theoretical. And if your engineering culture was low-trust before AI, AI makes it worse, not better — fix the culture first.

📌 Simon Willison — Tech Lock-in Is Dead (And Datasette Gets an Agent)

A short post with a big implication for every architecture decision you make

Simon's been on a prolific May. His most thought-provoking piece was a short note (May 14) unpacking a Mitchell Hashimoto quote about Bun migrating from Zig to Rust: "Programming languages used to be LOCK IN, and they're increasingly not so."

Simon had just heard a similar story at a conference: a medium-sized tech company had completed a coding-agent-driven rewrite of both their iOS and Android apps to React Native. When he asked why React Native (given that agents lower the cost of maintaining separate native apps), the answer was: they chose it partly because if it turned out to be the wrong call, they could just port back to native in the future.

The implication: architectural lock-in used to be one of the most consequential long-term technology decisions a team made. Coding agents are eroding that moat. Rewrites that would have taken 18 months now take weeks. This changes the risk calculus for every "build vs. buy" and "which framework" decision on your roadmap.

Also notable: he shipped Datasette Agent 0.1a4 (May 21) — a working integration of Claude as an autonomous agent inside the Datasette data platform. Part of a pattern Simon is tracking: purpose-built agents embedded inside existing tools, rather than standalone chatbots. He also published "The Last Six Months in LLMs in Five Minutes" (May 19) — his best rapid-fire summaries are a consistent resource for staying calibrated.

💡 PM takeaway: "Technology lock-in risk" deserves repricing on your risk register. When evaluating platform or framework choices, the migration cost is lower than it has ever been. This changes how you should weigh speed-to-market vs. future optionality — you can afford to be more decisive and less precious about reversibility.

📌 Harrison Chase — Context Engineering Is the New AI Moat

LangChain turns 3, and the framing shifts from building agents to operating them

Harrison marked LangChain's third birthday (May 5) with a reflective post and major product announcements: LangChain 1.0, LangSmith expansion, $125M in new funding, 1B+ downloads. But the more important shift is conceptual.

His framing of where the industry stands in 2026: the question is no longer "Can you build an agent?" It's "Can you observe, evaluate, govern, and operate agents reliably in production?" This is a fundamentally different problem — less about scaffolding, more about reliability engineering for systems that make autonomous decisions.

The Sequoia interview distilled his core thesis: context engineering is the new moat for long-horizon agents. Better models alone won't get your agent to production. What matters is how well you architect the context the agent operates in — what it can see, remember, retrieve, and decide. His new "Agent Harness/Deep Agents" tooling from Interrupt 2026 is aimed directly at this: control over orchestration, memory, and context in production deployments.

"Models will get better. Context engineering is what separates teams that ship agents that work from teams that demo agents that work."
💡 PM takeaway: If you're building AI features that involve agents, "context engineering" is the discipline your team needs to develop. This means thinking less about which model to use and more about what your agent can see, what it remembers, how it retrieves information, and how you'll know when it's degrading. The infra layer is becoming a real differentiator.

📌 Ethan Mollick — The Mass Intelligence Era Is Here

1 billion users, $0.14/million tokens, and institutions built for scarcity

Ethan's "Mass Intelligence" essay (the richest piece in his recent archive) documents a structural shift that changes the product strategy question: powerful AI has become a public utility. GPT-4 launched at $50 per million tokens. GPT-5 Nano — substantially more capable — is now $0.14 per million tokens. That's a 350x price drop with capability improvements. Google reported 33x energy efficiency improvement per prompt in a single year.

The implication isn't just cost. It's that the marginal cost of serving each additional user has collapsed, which means more business models become viable, free users can access reasoning that used to cost real money, and every assumption about "AI as a premium feature" needs to be revisited.

"Every institution we have — schools, hospitals, courts, companies, governments — was built for a world where intelligence was scarce and expensive. Now every profession, every institution, every community has to figure out how to thrive with Mass Intelligence."

His most recent post (April 23, "Sign of the Future: GPT-5.5") continues this thread: models are advancing faster than we're developing the interfaces and norms to use them well. The gap between what models can do and what average users are asking them to do is widening — which is both a product opportunity and a design challenge.

💡 PM takeaway: "Intelligence is now a commodity input" should be a first-principles assumption in every product strategy you write. The question isn't whether your product can use AI — it's whether you've rethought your product's core value prop for a world where users can already do the cognitive work you used to do for them. If your moat is "we analyze X faster," that moat is gone.

📌 Swyx (Shawn Wang) — The Tiny Teams Playbook

Universal advice from 7 teams with 100 people and $200M ARR

Swyx published his Tiny Teams Playbook on Latent.Space — synthesizing patterns from high-efficiency, AI-native teams achieving extraordinary revenue per employee. His definition of a Tiny Team: "more ARR in millions than employees." Efficiency as the governing principle, not headcount as a signal of ambition.

The core playbook principles:

  • Simple, boring tech stack: Shell scripts over Kubernetes. Start from a UI wrapper over one API call to an LLM and add complexity only when forced. Modularity as a discipline, not a feature.
  • Invest in evals early: Top-tier internal evals are a competitive moat. The teams that win know when their system degrades before customers do.
  • Compound learning: Every problem solved should generate a reusable artifact — template, playbook, prompt — that makes the next similar problem faster. Knowledge compounds; re-solving the same problem doesn't.
  • The AI Engineer was the single-player game. Tiny Teams is co-op multiplayer — more adaptive, more resilient, capable of far more.
💡 PM takeaway: Use this as a diagnostic. If your team of 12 can't outperform what a well-equipped Tiny Team of 5 could do, you likely have a process and tooling problem, not a resource problem. The "more people = more output" equation is breaking down. Where specifically is your team's leverage low?

🔥 This Week's Deep Pull: Elena Verna's 6-Tier AI-Nativeness Framework

The single most actionable thing from this week's research is Elena Verna's 6-tier framework for AI-nativeness, published via Lenny's Newsletter on May 21. It's the clearest mental model circulating right now for where you actually are on AI adoption — not where you claim to be.

Most PMs are at Tier 1-2 (writing buddy, meeting notes). The Tier 3 jump — using AI as a genuine thinking partner that challenges your logic rather than validates it — is where the real leverage shift happens. The prompt engineering required is counterintuitive: you have to explicitly demand disagreement. LLMs will agree with you by default. "Disagree with me. What's the strongest counter-argument?" is the prompt that transforms a yes-machine into a thinking partner. This is especially critical for strategy work, where the default AI behavior is actively dangerous.

The Tier 5-6 leap (shipping to prod, running agents) is where PMs become genuinely irreplaceable rather than endangered — because the people who can define what the agent should do, verify its outputs, and manage its failure modes are exactly the people with product sense and domain context. The technical threshold is lower than ever; the PM judgment layer has never been more valuable.

For AI-native PM thinking specifically: this framework is your new career self-assessment tool. Not "am I using AI?" but "which tier am I operating at, and what would it take to move up one tier this quarter?" One tier of growth per quarter compounds quickly.
