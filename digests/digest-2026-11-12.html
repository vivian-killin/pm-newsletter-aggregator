How to Survive AI as a SaaS Company
TL;DR — PM TakeawayAI commoditizes version one of your product—survival depends on betting strategically on what comes after. Webflow CEO Linda Tong's three-bet framework (know where you stand, build the right team, pick your bets) shows that public markets reward SaaS companies that move beyond generic AI integration to defensible differentiation.
Read more

The SaaS market is more volatile than headlines suggest. While the talk of "SaaS-pocalypse" is overblown, investors are ruthless about companies that haven't articulated a clear AI strategy. Atlassian reported 24% revenue growth and 31% cloud growth—yet dropped 17% after earnings because the market sees no differentiation strategy.

Webflow's approach offers a template: first, diagnose where your product stands relative to AI commoditization. Second, assemble a team that can execute on intentional bets rather than reactive pivots. Third, pick which customers will pay for your AI-powered differentiation (personalization, workflow automation, domain expertise) versus which will migrate to free AI tools. The key insight is that "getting paid for everything after version one" requires deciding what your moat actually is in an AI-native world.

Peter Yang

When to Use Fable 5.2 vs Opus 5.5 in Claude Code (Full Tutorial)
TL;DR — PM TakeawayModel selection is now a core product design decision. Yang's breakdown of when to deploy Fable (faster, cheaper, real-time interactions) versus Opus (complex reasoning, high-stakes decisions) shows that AI-native products require deliberate trade-off thinking at the feature level, not just infrastructure.
Read more

As Claude Code and similar agent frameworks mature, the choice of model is no longer purely an engineering optimization—it's a product strategy question. Yang demonstrates this through five real projects: a chief-of-staff agent (Opus for complex prioritization), an advisor (Fable for responsive interactions), workflow automation (Fable for cost), mobile app building (Opus for reasoning), and care coordination (Opus for safety).

The pattern reveals that faster, cheaper models work best for high-frequency, lower-stakes interactions where the user expects real-time feedback. Larger models are essential when the cost of hallucination is high or when the reasoning chain is complex. PMs building agent-native products need to think through this trade-off explicitly in their feature design, not leave it as a pure infrastructure decision.

Teresa Torres

Nov 10
Discovery & The 4 Risks - All Things Product Podcast with Teresa Torres & Petra Wille
TL;DR — PM TakeawayEven as AI reshapes how we build, the core discipline of continuous discovery has four failure modes that AI amplifies: misunderstanding the customer, building the wrong thing, scaling prematurely, and losing organizational buy-in. Treating discovery itself as a product—with its own customer (organizations) and value metrics—reveals why discovery breaks down under AI hype.
Read more

Torres and Wille flip the lens by asking: if discovery is a practice that organizations buy into (through training, adoption, habit change), what risks cause that "product" to fail in the market? The four risks emerge: companies misdiagnose what customers actually need, build solutions to the wrong problem, scale prematurely without evidence, or fail to sustain organizational commitment once initial enthusiasm fades.

This framing is particularly relevant in an AI-first era, where speed of iteration can mask deeper discovery failures. The takeaway for PMs: don't let AI velocity become an excuse to skip the hard work of understanding customer jobs, constraints, and trade-offs. The same rigor that makes discovery work in traditional software is even more critical when agents and automations multiply the ways a product can fail silently.

Swyx (Richard MacManus)

Nov 12
The Jev Revolution: What Engineers Are Already Building with System One Models
TL;DR — PM TakeawaySystem-one models (fast, narrow, cost-efficient) are shifting from lab curiosities to production workhorses. The real-world wins from Glean, Resend, and others show that agent design no longer requires a reasoning model for every task—PMs need to think in terms of routing, cost-per-interaction, and user experience trade-offs across model tiers.
Read more

At AI Engineer Code Summit, the pattern became clear: companies are moving beyond "use the biggest model" toward deliberate model selection for different tasks within the same agent workflow. Glean showed how they use lighter models for retrieval and classification, reserving heavier reasoning for synthesis. Resend demonstrated that API design for agents (including tool definitions, step-by-step execution, and cloud handoffs) is now as important as API design for humans.

The implications for product teams: system-one model proliferation means you can't treat "the model" as a black box. You need to think about task routing, cost budgeting across a user session, latency expectations, and fallback behavior when lightweight models hit their limits. This is especially critical for applications where users interact with agents in tight loops—the economics and UX only work if you've deliberately chosen the right model for each step.
