Teresa Torres

Sep 29
Is innovation the goal — or just a byproduct of solving customer problems well?
TL;DR — PM TakeawayStop chasing innovation as an end in itself. Torres argues that real innovation emerges from deeply understanding and solving customer problems — which means your discovery process, not your mission statement, determines whether you actually innovate.
Read more

In this episode of All Things Product, Teresa Torres and Petra Wille unpack one of product's most misused words: "innovation." The conversation challenges the common framing where innovation is treated as a standalone goal or a box to tick on a roadmap.

Torres makes the case that innovation is a natural outcome of solving customer problems exceptionally well. When teams invest in continuous discovery and customer empathy, they uncover non-obvious solutions that feel genuinely novel. The inverse is also true: teams that treat innovation as a separate mandate, divorced from customer context, tend to build features no one needs.

Oct 4
OpenAI's Head of ChatGPT on the agent era, why Dots is their biggest bet, and what builders are still getting wrong.
TL;DR — PM TakeawayTibo Sottiaux signals that the next phase of AI products isn't about better chat interfaces—it's about agents that operate autonomously on your behalf. PMs should stop designing task-by-task flows and start thinking about delegation, trust, and verification in agent systems.
Read more

Sottiaux frames the current moment as a transition from "AI as tool" to "AI as agent." He argues that agents will increasingly handle entire workflows without explicit user prompting, and that this shift will dominate how users interact with the internet. This has direct implications for product strategy: traditional UI patterns (buttons, forms, linear workflows) become less relevant when users delegate decisions to autonomous systems.

He also signals that OpenAI's "Dots" project represents their biggest structural bet for the next era. While long on vision, the interview emphasizes that most builders are still designing products around interaction rather than delegation—a fundamental category error for the AI-native products that will win.

Peter Yang

How Granola's co-founder uses AI habits to show up prepared and avoid AI slop in team workflows.
TL;DR — PM TakeawaySam Stephenson's practical framework shows how to embed AI into team operations—meeting prep, note synthesis, follow-ups—without burning out on low-value prompting or drowning in mediocre outputs. The discipline is in knowing where AI adds signal and where it introduces noise.
Read more

Stephenson walks through concrete AI habits that Granola uses: preparing for meetings with synthesis of prior context, capturing live notes during calls, and automating follow-ups without the team having to manually iterate on AI drafts. The key insight is about systemic integration rather than point solutions.

He also addresses the underrated problem of AI burnout—when teams spend more time reviewing and fixing AI outputs than they would have spent on the work itself. The solution isn't to use AI everywhere; it's to be surgical about where human-in-the-loop workflows actually save time and reduce decision friction.

Marty Cagan

Apr 9, 2026
Worth your time regardless of when it was published
Why it's worth your timeThe distinction between commercial and internal products forces you to rethink org structure, team incentives, and what "success" means. Both are hard, but internal products often get deprioritized and underfunded—yet they directly enable your ability to serve customers.
Read more

Cagan argues that internal products—tools, platforms, and services your own teams use to serve customers—deserve the same rigor and investment as commercial products. Yet they rarely get it. Internal tools often live in organizational limbo: not quite a cost center, not quite a revenue driver, and staffed with whoever can be spared.

The insight for AI-native teams is sharp: as you automate and delegate more work to agents and systems, the internal tools your teams use to oversee, verify, and iterate on those systems become critical competitive advantages. Treating internal product as a second-class citizen will slow your ability to ship and scale AI products effectively.

Rich Mironov

Apr 17, 2026
Worth your time regardless of when it was published
Why it's worth your timeMost AI product economics are still built on the assumption that token costs are negligible. Mironov flags the underappreciated crisis: as your users scale, so do your inference costs—and your margin math breaks. This is the next frontier for AI product thinking.
Read more

Mironov highlights an often-overlooked layer of AI product economics: runtime token costs. Teams get excited about the acceleration AI brings to development and feature shipping, but many haven't modeled the cost structure of serving AI features at scale. As adoption rises, so do LLM API costs—and unlike traditional software, there's no easy economy of scale.

For PMs building AI products, this means you can't treat inference costs as an implementation detail to hand off to engineering. You need to model unit economics, understand your token-per-transaction ratio, and design features with cost-efficiency as a first-class constraint, not an afterthought.
