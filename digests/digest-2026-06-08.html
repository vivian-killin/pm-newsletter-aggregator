Building taste and shipping products that last in the AI era
TL;DR — PM TakeawayJudgment and taste—not just data—drive exceptional products. Fadell's "three generations rule" (building for today, tomorrow, and a decade out) is a framework for navigating AI-powered feature abundance and avoiding short-term optimization traps.
Read more

Tony Fadell's career at Apple and Nest teaches a hard lesson: the best products aren't built by chasing metrics or letting AI generate every possible feature. Instead, Fadell emphasizes the role of human judgment in deciding what matters. He argues that PMs need to develop taste—the ability to see what users will love before they know they want it.

His "three generations rule" applies to product decisions: build for today (ship working features), tomorrow (next cycle iteration), and a decade out (architectural resilience). In an age where AI can generate a thousand feature ideas in minutes, this framework becomes a decision-making guard rail. The implication for AI-native product teams: more velocity doesn't mean better products without intentional, opinionated choices about what to say no to.

Sandboxing and agentic text editing—building safe, editable AI workflows
TL;DR — PM TakeawayFor AI agents that edit content (SQL, Markdown, SVG), design matters as much as capability. Willison's datasette-agent-edit plugin shows how to build trust in agentic workflows by giving users visibility and control over edits—a critical UX pattern for PM teams building AI-powered editing tools.
Read more

Simon Willison is expanding the Datasette Agent ecosystem with tools for safe, sandboxed code execution and agentic editing of text. His micropython-wasm release offers a way to run Python in a sandboxed WASM environment—useful for platforms that need to let users execute code without risk. The datasette-agent-edit plugin addresses a subtler challenge: how do you let an AI agent edit existing documents (SQL queries, Markdown files, SVG graphics) while maintaining user trust?

The design pattern he cites from Claude's text editor is instructive: agents need view, edit, and approval tools built into the interaction model, not bolted on after. For PMs building agentic features, this is a reminder that capability alone (agent can edit) isn't enough—you need workflow design that keeps humans in the loop and transparent about what the agent changed.

Why your broken RL environments are actively harming your models
TL;DR — PM TakeawayFor teams building AI agents via reinforcement learning, the quality of training environments is non-negotiable. A poorly designed reward signal or broken simulation doesn't just slow progress—it actively teaches models to behave badly. This shifts the PM focus from "how many environments can we test?" to "how do we validate environment quality?"
Read more

Swyx cuts through a common blind spot in AI-native product development: the assumption that more training trajectories always equal better models. In reality, low-quality RL environments—with broken reward signals, unrealistic physics, or poorly designed task specifications—actively degrade model performance. The problem is subtle because the model will still train; it just learns to exploit quirks in the broken environment rather than solving the real problem.

The PM implication is straightforward but often overlooked: discovery and validation of your training environment matters as much as discovery of user problems. Before scaling your agent training pipeline, do a quality audit of your reward design, simulation accuracy, and environment specifications. Bad environments are expensive feedback loops.
