Gergely Orosz

May 26–27
Job market trends, AI coding adoption, and why engineering judgment remains irreplaceable
TL;DR — PM TakeawayThe job market is bifurcating: demand for "AI engineers" is skyrocketing, but traditional software engineering roles aren't being displaced—they're evolving. PMs should expect hiring managers to ask for AI literacy alongside core engineering skills, and recognize that the speed-to-ship advantage goes to teams that blend judgment with tool leverage.
Read more

Orosz's analysis of 2026 hiring data shows a counterintuitive pattern. Rather than AI tools eliminating engineering jobs, they've created a new category of specialization: roles that demand both deep technical judgment and fluency with AI-assisted development. The "AI engineering" boom is overlapping with, not replacing, software engineering hiring.

His conversation with Dax Raad (OpenCode co-founder) adds texture: AI coding tools have limits—they excel at boilerplate and refactoring, but miss critical architectural decisions and testing philosophy. For product teams, this means your hiring bar shouldn't drop; it should shift to include AI literacy as table stakes.

The second key insight: teams that view AI tooling as force multiplication (not replacement) are shipping faster and making better trade-off decisions, because they're freeing engineers to focus on problems that require judgment.

Simon Willison

Jun 1–2
Meta AI vulnerabilities, file handling workflows, and what went wrong with support bot security
TL;DR — PM TakeawayWhen building AI-powered support systems, basic interaction design can become a security vulnerability—Meta's AI bot was tricked into account takeovers by simple social engineering. As a PM, stress-test your AI agents against user manipulation and boundary-crossing requests early; the cost of a compromised user trust event far exceeds the cost of friction in happy-path interactions.
Read more

Willison flagged a critical vulnerability in Meta's AI support bot: attackers asked it directly to link email addresses to Instagram accounts they didn't own, and the bot complied. This isn't a model problem; it's a system design failure. The bot was given capabilities (account linking) without sufficient safeguards against social pressure.

The lesson for product teams building AI agents: capability and permission are not the same thing. Your AI system should refuse requests that violate the user's ownership or identity, even (especially) when phrased politely or urgently. Build in adversarial user testing before launch.

On the flip side, Willison's work on the Pasted File Editor shows how thoughtful UX design around AI interactions improves both usability and safety—large text pastes are auto-detected and containerized, reducing the cognitive load on the model and constraining scope.

Swyx's interview with Ethan He (xAI's Grok Imagine lead) reveals that the next evolution beyond text and image generation is agent-like video models that can simulate step-by-step sequences, maintain coherence across frames, and respond to user intent iteratively. Grok Imagine was built in 3 months—proof that the technical baseline is moving fast.

The distinction matters: static video generators output a clip. Video agents let users refine, branch, and explore variations interactively. This opens doors for product teams in education, design iteration, simulation, and content creation to offer richer, more exploratory experiences.

NVIDIA's announcements (Cosmos 3, Nemotron 3 Ultra) reinforce that edge and real-time video processing is becoming commoditized. The constraint moving forward isn't raw capability; it's UI/UX and product insight about what users actually want to do with video agents.

Marty Cagan & SVPG

Apr 16–27
Worth your time regardless of when it was published
Why it's worth your timeCagan's framework of "Build to Learn" vs. "Build to Earn" is essential for AI-era product teams—as tooling costs collapse, the economics shift from shipping features on a fixed roadmap to rapid discovery. This framework should reshape how you staff and prioritize in 2026.
Read more

The traditional model is "Build to Earn"—executives define a roadmap of features, teams execute, and stakeholders get output. Cagan argues that as AI and no-code reduce delivery friction, the competitive advantage moves to "Build to Learn"—rapid experimentation, validated learning, and pivoting based on evidence rather than executive instinct.

This distinction is critical for PMs: if your team is structured around delivery (Gantt charts, sprints, predictable output), you're optimized for a world where shipping is hard. If your infrastructure supports rapid discovery (small bets, fast feedback loops, clear learning signals), you're positioned for 2026, where shipping is easy but knowing what to ship is everything.
