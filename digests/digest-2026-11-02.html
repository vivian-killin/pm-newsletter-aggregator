Basil Chatha

Evals without traces, million-cell tabular reviews, and cracks in the billable hour as a business model
TL;DR — PM TakeawayWhen you cannot inspect agent behavior due to data ownership constraints, you must invert your eval strategy: instead of tracing execution, focus on business outcomes and sampled audits. This shifts PM thinking from observability-first to privacy-first agent design.
Read more

Legora builds AI agents for law firms but faces a unique constraint: it cannot read the data or traces of what its agents do because that data belongs to the law firm's clients under confidentiality agreements. This eliminates traditional debugging and eval methods that rely on full execution visibility.

The piece explores how Legora adapted its product development around this constraint—using tabular outcome reviews, sampled audits, and indirect performance signals rather than direct trace inspection. The conversation also touches on how AI is beginning to reshape the billable-hour business model itself, forcing legal tech to rethink pricing and value capture.

Jaclyn Konzelmann, working in Google Labs, discusses how Gemini 4's emergence has changed product development at Google. With AI now capable of generating and reasoning about complex code, the traditional moat of specialized engineering expertise has eroded. This forces PMs to pivot: instead of treating code as a defensible competitive advantage, they must focus on architectural choices, taste, and problem-solving scope that AI cannot yet navigate independently.

The conversation spans how this affects hiring, skill development, and the kinds of problems worth solving. Konzelmann also shares how she uses AI with her own children, illustrating the generational shift in tool adoption.

Worth your time regardless of when it was published
Why it's worth your timeGilad systematically diagnoses why product discovery breaks down in practice—not as theory but as organizational dysfunction. Three core challenges are rooted in how product orgs structure themselves, not in methodology alone.
Read more

In part two of his discovery series, Gilad moves beyond generic best practices to examine what actually breaks discovery in real organizations. He identifies three systemic challenges specific to how product teams operate: dysfunction in discovery prioritization, weak integration between research and delivery, and organizational incentives that reward shipping over learning. Each stems from how the product org itself is structured and managed, not from PMs simply failing to apply discovery techniques well enough.

This framework is especially relevant now as AI hype accelerates the pressure to ship fast and skip the discovery phase entirely. Understanding where discovery actually fails helps leaders defend rigorous discovery practices even when velocity culture dominates.
