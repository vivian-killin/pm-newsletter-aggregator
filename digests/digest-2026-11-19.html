Itamar Gilad

Nov 17
Five ways teams misuse ICE scoring and how to fix them
TL;DR — PM TakeawayICE scoring's simplicity is also its trap—teams often ignore confidence bands, multiply scores mechanically, and skip the deeper conversation. Gilad shows how to use ICE as a conversation starter, not a black box, and when to override it entirely.
Read more

Gilad identifies five antipatterns in how teams implement ICE (Impact, Confidence, Ease) scoring. The core issue: teams treat the final score as gospel, when the framework is really designed to surface disagreement and force discussion. Common mistakes include scoring confidence as if it's binary, using the same scale for unrelated projects, and treating multiplication as the only way to rank ideas.

His fix is to use ICE as a conversation tool, not a decision algorithm. High-confidence, high-impact ideas should be prioritized, but the disagreement on confidence or impact across team members is where the actual learning happens. Sometimes the lower-scoring idea wins because it unblocks something bigger, or because one person's expertise shifts the team's true confidence level.

John Cutler

Nov 18
Contexting vs. context: why senior people struggle to describe what they know
TL;DR — PM TakeawayCutler distinguishes between active contexting (the process of gathering and synthesizing information) and static context (the facts themselves)—and shows how even expert PMs often skip the work of making their reasoning visible to others. This matters because distributed AI teams need context, not just conclusions.
Read more

Cutler observes that highly experienced people are often bad at explaining their expertise. They've internalized so much context—customer signals, failed experiments, roadmap constraints—that they jump to conclusions without laying out the intermediate reasoning. When they say "we should do X," they're drawing on years of pattern recognition, but the person listening just hears "do X" without understanding why.

In an AI-native workflow, this gap matters even more. If you're prompting an agent to make a decision, you need to encode not just the facts but the reasoning framework. That means senior PMs need to get better at contexting—the active process of making their thinking visible and reproducible, not just dumping their conclusions into a Slack channel.
