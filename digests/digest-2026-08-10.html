Marty Cagan

August 10
Establishing what the product role actually requires in modern product organizations.
TL;DR — PM Takeaway

Operating models—not titles—determine what PMs need to do. Cagan argues for clarity on whether you're building in an optimization model (incremental value through discovery) or a transformation model (breakthrough innovation). Your responsibilities, team structure, and success metrics shift dramatically based on which one you're in. Define your model first; the job description follows.

Read more

Cagan shows that "product manager," "product owner," and similar titles have become nearly meaningless because organizations don't agree on what they mean. The real differentiator is operating model: teams either optimize existing products and platforms (incremental discovery and prioritization) or pursue transformational goals that require deep innovation and market creation.

In an optimization model, you manage roadmaps, run discovery, prioritize features, and measure incremental growth. In a transformation model, you immerse yourself in the problem space, build deep user relationships, prototype relentlessly, and define success by learning and product-market fit signals—not velocity or quarterly metrics.

The AI context amplifies this distinction. AI-native products rarely start with clear feature lists; they require deeper discovery about what problems AI actually solves for users. Your model determines whether you can do that work effectively.

Simon Willison

August 10
AI agents used to hack a real-world booking system with zero authorization checks.
TL;DR — PM Takeaway

This is a cautionary tale for shipping AI-powered features: authorization logic must be explicit and enforced at the API level, not assumed. OpenClaw (an AI security researcher) used an LLM agent to test an Australian gym-booking system and successfully canceled other users' reservations because the cancellation endpoint had no permission checks. Before shipping any agent-powered feature touching user data or transactions, audit your API security—assume agents will test every path.

Read more

OpenClaw discovered that the gym-booking platform's API allowed canceling reservations without verifying ownership or permissions. A simple LLM agent probing the API found the vulnerability and exploited it. This demonstrates a real gap between human-centered API design and agent-ready API design: humans might not discover or attempt such tests in normal UX flows, but agents will.

For product teams building AI agents that interact with external APIs or your own services, this is a critical reminder: security and authorization assumptions don't hold at agent scale. Agents execute systematically; they don't have the human hesitation to "try that."

Grace Clarke rebuilt her entire service business as a Claude-powered automation workflow.
TL;DR — PM Takeaway

This is what AI-native workflow design looks like: Grace automated 20 hours of weekly admin work (proposals, client tracking, email) into a single Claude-powered pipeline. The PM insight isn't the code—it's how she thought about bundling disconnected manual tasks into one coherent AI system. She didn't automate each task independently; she automated the *entire process flow*. That's the mental model shift for building AI products: look for end-to-end workflows, not feature automation.

Read more

Grace's workflow runs end-to-end: prospects fill a form, Claude generates a customized proposal, logs the prospect data, and sends personalized email follow-ups. One integrated system replaces a fragmented set of manual tools. The outcome: 20 hours of weekly admin eliminated, more time for client relationships and business growth.

For PMs: This shows the difference between automating a feature and automating a workflow. Grace didn't build "a proposal generator" or "an email writer"—she built a system that reasons across the entire business process. That's where AI creates leverage. The implication for your roadmap: look for workflows that span multiple tools or manual handoffs. Those are your targets for AI-native redesign.

Swyx

August 8
Zawinski's Law applies to multi-agent systems: every agent tries to become a full platform.
TL;DR — PM Takeaway

Swyx connects an old design principle (Zawinski's Law: "Every program attempts to expand until it can read mail") to modern multi-agent systems. Single-purpose agents are unstable; they drift toward becoming full platforms. For PMs building agent-based products, recognize that agents naturally want broader scope, which creates scope creep and fragmentation. Design your system architecture and incentives to constrain agent scope explicitly, or watch your agent ecosystem sprawl.

Read more

Zawinski's Law is an old programmer observation: software tends to expand beyond its original scope to duplicate functionality found in other tools. In agent systems, this manifests as agents attempting to do things outside their domain because they have access to APIs and reasoning capability. The result is sprawl, redundancy, and a chaotic multi-agent ecosystem.

The implications for AI product design are significant. You need explicit boundaries and role definitions for agents, clear API constraints, and careful orchestration logic. Otherwise, agents will compete, duplicate work, and make your system harder to reason about.

Julie Zhuo

August 5
Worth your time regardless of when it was published
Why it's worth your time

Julie writes a letter to her daughter about the paradox of AI making things easy: the easier tools become, the less valuable the act of *making* becomes. The deeper insight for PMs is about user psychology and behavior design. If your AI product removes all friction and challenge, you might also remove motivation and satisfaction. How do you design AI systems that empower and delight, not just automate?

Read more

Julie reflects on how technological ease can become a trap. When creating something requires no effort or skill, it often loses meaning—both for the creator and the audience. She asks: if an AI does the work, what have you actually learned? What have you created?

For product teams, this questions the default assumption that frictionless = good. Some friction—the friction of learning, experimenting, iterating—is what makes creation feel worthwhile. The best AI products augment human effort, not erase it entirely. They should preserve the parts of the process that matter to users psychologically.
