Teresa Torres

Nov 24
In a world of AI products, what even counts as a bug — and can you still promise to fix it?
TL;DR — PM TakeawayAI products break the traditional bug model because probabilistic outputs mean the same input can produce different results. You need new language and frameworks for how you talk to customers about quality, regression, and what "fixed" actually means in a system where non-determinism is a feature, not a flaw.
Read more

Torres and Wille explore the uncomfortable reality that AI-powered features don't fail in classical ways. A hallucination isn't reproducible like a database crash; a model's drift is gradual and contextual. This forces PMs to rethink their QA contracts with users, their internal definitions of severity, and how to handle the gap between "this output was wrong" and "this output was always going to be wrong sometimes."

The conversation surfaces how teams are starting to separate deterministic bugs (the LLM returned an empty response) from probabilistic failures (the LLM returned something coherent but factually incorrect). That distinction changes your roadmap, your incident response, and your communication strategy.

Peter Yang

How I built a free skill that turns any topic into a 1-minute explainer video using Gemini 4 Neon and Antigravity
TL;DR — PM TakeawayYang demonstrates a working pattern for composing AI capabilities—Orchestrator, voice mode, browser automation, and swarm agents—into a cohesive user workflow. The step-by-step breakdown shows how to chain agents without exposing complexity, turning a multi-step technical problem into a single user action, which is the core PM challenge in AI product design.
Read more

Yang walks through building an AI video explainer skill from first principles using Google's latest models. The architecture layers voice input, topic understanding, research via browser automation, and video generation—each handled by a specialized agent or model. The key insight for PMs is how the orchestration layer hides that complexity; the user asks once and gets a finished 1-minute video without manually routing between tools.

This is a template for how to think about AI product composition: define the user's end state, map the sub-tasks that need to happen, then pick which model or agent handles each one. Yang's example works through the actual trade-offs (which Gemini model for which step, when to use voice vs text, how swarms parallelize work) so you can apply the same reasoning to your own workflows.

Swyx

Nov 26
How Clay Builds GTM Agents — Jeff Barg, Head of AI
TL;DR — PM TakeawayBarg explains why Clay's core insight—making AI agents cheap enough to deploy at scale while keeping the UX intentional—flips the traditional trade-off between automation and control. The gotcha: users hate model selectors; they want you to pick the right tool for them. This shapes decisions about opaque orchestration, pricing models, and how you hide complexity in agent products.
Read more

Clay is building infrastructure that lets companies deploy AI agents for go-to-market functions at a cost low enough to make one agent per workflow feasible ($1,500 to qualify entire company segments). The strategy sidesteps the agent-builder arms race by building specifically for GTM, where you can optimize for concrete business outcomes rather than general-purpose reasoning.

Barg surfaces a sharp UX principle: customers don't want to choose between Claude, GPT, or Gemini. They want you to have already chosen the right model for the task and hidden it. This pushes agent products away from transparency and toward opinionated defaults, with just enough knobs for power users. The implication for PMs: in agent products, simplicity often means wrapping complexity better, not exposing more of it.

TL;DR — PM TakeawayShihipar shows that the future isn't Revolution (AI replaces humans) or Renaissance (humans refine AI output)—it's both running in parallel. You can generate a thousand variations cheaply while spending focused time on one that matters. The PM angle: this reshapes your roadmap granularity and forces you to think about which 1% of your surface area deserves craft versus which 99% can be delegated to generative systems.
Read more

Speaking at AI Engineer CODE, Shihipar uses a fighting game as an example: AI generates hundreds of rock sizes and placements; a human artist tweaks the one that ships. This isn't AI replacing design; it's AI handling the combinatorial explosion so humans can focus on taste and intention. For product, it means you can iterate on scale (ship variations, gather signal) and depth (perfect the one that wins) in the same cycle.
