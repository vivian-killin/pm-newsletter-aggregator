Gergely Orosz

June 02
Devs are generating twice as much code (or more) than just 6 months ago, which is a problem for quality, reliability, and tech debt.
TL;DR — PM TakeawaySet velocity expectations differently for AI-accelerated teams. The paradox: developers who pause to think critically about what they're asking agents to build produce better outcomes than those who accept every suggestion. This means your roadmap velocity metrics need recalibration—more output doesn't equal more shipped value.
Read more

Orosz documents a real phenomenon: AI coding agents have fundamentally shifted how quickly code moves from idea to commit. The challenge isn't speed—it's intentionality. Teams that treat AI agents as thoughtless accelerators pile up tech debt faster than they can ship features. Teams that use agents as thinking partners (asking the right questions before accepting suggestions) maintain quality while moving faster than before.

For PMs, this means rethinking how you measure progress. Commits, lines of code, or even feature count aren't good proxies anymore. Instead, focus on: Are we shipping fewer bugs? Are we reducing time-to-stability? Are we maintaining architectural coherence? The teams winning with AI agents are the ones who've internalized that a slower, more deliberate approach to code generation produces faster overall product delivery.

Ethan Mollick

June 04
Also: how pitch a book to an AI!
TL;DR — PM TakeawayMollick argues the era of co-intelligence (human + AI working in tandem) is ending; we're entering co-existence where AIs solve whole problem classes independently. Products that still frame AI as an "assistant" are already behind. Start designing for scenarios where users need the human OR the AI, not both.
Read more

This is a conceptual shift worth internalizing. Co-intelligence assumed humans would remain in control, overseeing AI suggestions. Co-existence acknowledges that for many tasks, the AI is simply the better choice—full stop. No human review needed. Mollick's framing has immediate implications for how you think about AI feature design.

Instead of "AI assists your workflow," ask: "For which parts of my user's journey does the AI become the primary actor?" Then design the handoff points (where humans take back control) intentionally and sparingly. This changes your product spec, UX patterns, and even success metrics. Products designed for co-existence will look very different from products built on co-intelligence assumptions.

Swyx and the Andon Labs team discuss how to evaluate LLMs in ways that actually predict production performance. The key insight: traditional benchmarks (MMLU, GSM8K) don't correlate well with whether a model will perform well on your actual product. VendingBench instead tests models on real vending machine customer service scenarios—messy, contextual, failure-prone situations.

This matters for your roadmap because it means you should invest in building internal evals tied to your product's actual workflows. Rather than adopting the latest model because it's #1 on some leaderboard, you'll want to run it against a suite of scenarios your users actually encounter. This requires a small upfront investment but pays dividends when choosing between models or deciding when to upgrade.

Ethan Mollick

Worth your time regardless of when it was published
Why it's worth your timeMollick surfaces a troubling pattern: social feeds are filling with AI-generated content that looks suspiciously uniform. This is the canary in the coal mine for product design—once AI can generate "good enough" at scale, human authenticity becomes the premium. Your product needs a deliberate stance on synthetic vs. authentic content, or your users will feel the degradation.
Read more

The argument is simple but powerful: as AI-generated content becomes easier and cheaper, the default trajectory is for feeds to fill with it. Users notice. Engagement drops. The product becomes less valuable. This isn't inevitable—it's a design choice. Platforms that explicitly curate for human-authored content, that surface signals of authenticity, or that let users filter synthetic content will win the trust battle. Those that remain agnostic or actively promote AI content will watch their communities thin out.
