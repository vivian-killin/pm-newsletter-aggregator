Itamar Gilad

Sep 24
Why Good Product Ideas Don't Survive
TL;DR — PM TakeawayGreat product ideas are rare, but they're killed by organizational dynamics—not by poor initial quality. Map the invisible forces in your org (politics, competing priorities, misaligned incentives) that doom strong concepts, then design explicit defense mechanisms into your discovery and roadmap processes.
Read more

Gilad argues that the best product ideas—those creating measurable value for users and business alike—are regularly abandoned not because they were bad, but because organizational friction, shifting priorities, and stakeholder misalignment systematically eliminate them. The ideas that survive tend to be politically safe or loudly championed by senior leaders, not necessarily the strongest ones.

For PMs, the implication is clear: protecting good ideas requires as much skill as identifying them. This means documenting why an idea was rejected (not just that it was), socializing concepts early across functions, and building the case for reconsideration as conditions change. In the AI era, where speed-to-ship has compressed, the risk of accidentally killing evergreen concepts because they didn't fit this quarter's narrative is higher than ever.

Teresa Torres

Sep 22
Trash Can Tracking – All Things Product Podcast
TL;DR — PM TakeawayTrack what ideas your team rejects and why—your trash can is a leading indicator of product process health, not your shipped features. This simple metric reveals whether you're exploring enough options, killing ideas too fast, or stuck in groupthink.
Read more

In this episode of All Things Product, Torres and Petra Wille introduce trash can tracking: a lightweight diagnostic for evaluating how many concepts your team explores before shipping. The premise is counterintuitive—a large "trash can" of rejected ideas often signals a healthy discovery process, while a tiny one suggests premature convergence or fear-driven decision-making.

For discovery-stage teams especially, this is a concrete way to measure whether you're truly exploring the solution space or narrowing too quickly. The metric also surfaces organizational problems: teams that never say no may lack decision criteria; teams that kill everything may be risk-averse or trapped by a dominant opinion.

Julie Zhuo

What will make personal AI go big? Four design principles behind Muse's appeal
TL;PR — PM TakeawayZhuo reverse-engineers why Muse succeeds where most AI apps fail: specificity over generality, genuine scarcity (it solves a real problem), habit formation by solving friction in an existing flow, and a narrow interface that reduces decision paralysis. Apply these four principles to any AI product roadmap.
Read more

Zhuo analyzes Muse—an AI note-taking and thought-organizing agent that launched this month to rare consumer enthusiasm—and distills four design principles that separate working AI products from the noise. First, it's deeply specific to a single job (organizing thoughts) rather than pretending to be a general assistant. Second, it solves a problem users actually felt acutely (thought capture feels scattered across tools). Third, it integrates into an existing behavior (note-taking) so adoption doesn't require new habits. Fourth, it has a simple, focused interface—no settings to debate, no endless options.

For PMs building AI-native products, this is a counterintuitive roadmap: the temptation is to add generality and optionality, but Zhuo's analysis suggests the opposite. Narrow beats broad, specific beats configurable, and friction reduction in an existing workflow beats a new mode of working.

Foundries vs Navigators: Lowering the Cost of Science
TL;DR — PM TakeawayAdrian Sanborn argues that AI has made thinking cheap but doing expensive in R&D. This shapes product strategy for any PM in life sciences, climate, or biotech: the bottleneck is no longer ideation—it's execution bandwidth and experimental validation. Build products that absorb the "doing," not just the thinking.
Read more

Sanborn explores how AI has inverted the cost structure of scientific research. Traditionally, thinking was expensive (requires elite talent) and doing was cheap (run an experiment). AI flipped it: generating hypotheses and design ideas is now nearly free, but the actual work of building, testing, and validating remains labor-intensive and capital-heavy. This asymmetry is reshaping how biotech and applied science companies operate.

For PMs in science-adjacent domains (biotech, climate tech, materials science), this is a critical insight: your moat isn't in idea generation—it's in execution infrastructure. Products that lower the cost of validation, parallelization, or feedback loops will outcompete those that just help teams brainstorm faster.

Why it's worth your timeCagan's Build to Learn FAQ unpacks the rising distinction between building to discover (product discovery) and building to ship (product delivery). As AI makes delivery cheaper and faster, the PM's leverage has shifted—the bottleneck is no longer speed, it's knowing what to build. This reframes the entire job.
Read more

Cagan argues that as the cost of product delivery drops—accelerated by AI-assisted engineering—the competitive advantage shifts from speed-to-ship to speed-to-insight. A PM's time is better spent on ruthless discovery, user interviews, and prototyping to learn than on being a delivery coordinator. In an age where AI can execute, the question "are we building the right thing?" matters more than "can we build it fast?"
