Marty Cagan

Sep 25
Why managers aren't becoming obsolete in the AI era—they're just being redefined.
TL;DR — PM TakeawayThe anti-manager sentiment misdiagnoses the problem. What's actually changing: the bar for what makes a good leader is rising, and AI removes excuses for mediocrity. Invest in developing skilled individual contributors who can lead without formal titles, and ensure your managers add demonstrable value—not just oversight.
Read more

Cagan pushes back on the common claim that AI will eliminate management layers. The real trend isn't fewer managers—it's a higher bar for what managers do. In an AI-native environment, the talented individual contributors can accomplish more on their own, which means managers must deliver clear expertise, judgment, and support that justifies their existence.

The implication for product teams: you can't hide weak managers or weak skill gaps behind process anymore. Your team will know immediately if a leader isn't adding value. The path forward is to develop expert individual contributors (both technical and product) who can mentor and lead laterally, and to make sure managers are genuine experts in their domain, not just coordinators.

Molly Graham

Sep 27
Why your old delegation playbook breaks down when the report is an AI system.
TL;DR — PM TakeawayDelegating to AI is categorically different from delegating to humans—you can't build trust, career growth, or mutual accountability the same way. The best managers are learning to keep certain high-judgment decisions on their plate (what Graham calls your "Legos") and being intentional about which tasks actually free up human time for higher-value work.
Read more

Graham revisits her famous "give away your Legos" framework and explains why it no longer works as stated. When you delegate to an AI system, you're not developing a person, building trust, or creating accountability the way you do with a human report. Instead, you're outsourcing execution without the reciprocal relationship that makes delegation healthy.

For product teams, this means being ruthless about which Legos to keep. Don't delegate your vision-setting, taste, or judgment calls to AI just because you can. Delegate the parts that genuinely free you up to do higher-leverage thinking. The trap: spending the time you saved on busywork instead of strategy.

Julie Zhuo

Sep 24
The design principles that make Muse feel like a real personal AI you'd actually use.
TL;DR — PM TakeawayZhuo identifies four design principles driving Muse's unusually high engagement: it feels proactive (not reactive), it surfaces surprises, it removes friction, and it respects your attention. If you're building any kind of AI-native consumer product, these patterns are worth studying—they explain why most AI apps feel like toys while Muse feels like a coworker.
Read more

Zhuo digs into why Muse—a consumer AI agent that just launched—has generated genuine enthusiasm in a crowded market of AI assistants. Most AI apps feel reactive: you ask them a question, they answer. Muse flips this. It brings you information and suggestions unprompted, learning your patterns and proactively handling low-stakes decisions.

The four principles: proactivity (it moves first), surprise (it finds things you didn't ask for), frictionlessness (it handles edge cases smoothly), and attention-respectfulness (it doesn't spam). These aren't novel individually, but their combination is rare in AI products. For PMs building agent-like experiences, Zhuo's breakdown offers a blueprint for moving beyond chatbot interactions into tools people actually form habits around.
