Emmanuel Ameisen

Oct 18
Inside the black box of AI: How AI models actually think
TL;DR — PM TakeawayModels perform sophisticated planning several tokens ahead without explicitly stating reasoning, and exhibit structures analogous to conscious/unconscious cognition. This changes how you should think about prompt design, error recovery, and building workflows that work with—not against—how models actually reason.
Read more

Ameisen, an interpretability researcher at Anthropic, shares findings from looking inside Claude and other LLMs. The key insight is that models form general concept representations and plan ahead in ways that the simple "next-token predictor" framing completely misses. Models have an internal "J space" that reveals something akin to self-awareness, and smooth scaling curves underlie what appear to be sudden capability jumps.

For product people, this matters because it suggests models aren't black boxes doing word completion—they're doing structured reasoning that can sometimes be audited and directed. Understanding this opens design space around how to build interfaces that make that reasoning visible or steerable, and how to design for failure modes that emerge from model planning rather than token randomness.

Teresa Torres

Oct 13
Training Expectations: Can one hour teach your team continuous discovery?
TL;DR — PM TakeawayA flat no—you cannot build a discovery practice in a single offsite session. Continuous discovery is a habit and team muscle that requires sustained, repeated practice over weeks and months. If you're planning a quick workshop, expect it to motivate but not transform behavior.
Read more

Torres and co-host Petra Wille discuss why the common approach of booking a one-hour training slot during an offsite doesn't work. Discovery isn't a concept you learn once; it's a practiced skill that only embeds when teams do it repeatedly in their actual work rhythms.

The implication for PMs: if you want your team to truly adopt discovery, budget for ongoing coaching and normalize it as part of sprint cadence, not a one-time training event. Short sessions can raise awareness, but behavioral change requires sustained repetition.

Swyx & Guests

Oct 14–13
Two new episodes: Lab-in-the-loop biology + hour-long autonomous robotics
TL;DR — PM TakeawayBoth episodes show how AI agents are moving from conversational tools into embodied, multi-step workflows—one in wet-lab biotech, one in physical robotics. PMs should watch for the shift from "AI as copilot" to "AI as autonomous worker," which changes product metrics, safety requirements, and feedback loops entirely.
Read more

Lab-in-the-Loop (Leash Bio): Ian Quigley explains how they tamed DEL (display library) assays—notoriously noisy data—and used it to catch model hallucinations, then fed that data back to train AI agents to think like chemists. The feedback loop between data, model evaluation, and agent training is the real product.

Dyna Robotics: Jason Ma's team is betting on World Action Models—learned representations of how the physical world responds to actions—as the foundation for general-purpose robots with hour-long autonomy. This is the embodied cousin of language models: learning to reason over time in a continuous domain.

Both point to a design pattern: successful AI products in science and robotics aren't shipping one-shot predictions; they're building tight loops where agent actions, real-world feedback, and model updates form a continuous cycle.

Julie Zhuo

Aug 5
Worth your time regardless of when it was published
Why it's worth your timeA meditation on the value of trying when AI makes things so easy. Zhuo writes to her 11-year-old daughter about why the journey of learning to draw or write matters more than the perfect output—and why her generation will need to redefine what effort and mastery mean.
Read more

Zhuo reflects on how AI commoditizes the output: anyone can generate a passable essay or image. But the act of trying—the struggle to translate thought into hand, eye, or word—builds something in the maker that the finished product alone never could. She anchors this in her own experience trying to become a novelist in college, and how that failure taught her more than success could have.

For PMs: this touches on a design question that will only deepen: if AI can do the thing, why does the human doing it still matter? How do you build products that honor both the efficiency AI brings and the deeper human need to create, learn, and struggle? Some products will lean into "let AI do it faster," but others will need to design around why the human process matters—and that's a harder, more durable product thesis.

Why it's worth your timeA clear-eyed assessment of whether AI has actually disrupted product development—or whether we're just adding hype to the same old problems. Worth reading before you restructure your org around AI.
Read more

Gilad cuts through the narrative that AI fundamentally changes how we develop and sell tech products. He examines whether the old patterns—discovery, validation, shipping—are still the bottleneck, and asks whether AI actually removes friction or just makes it more seductive to skip it. The piece is a corrective to anyone who believes AI is a shortcut to better products without deeper discovery work.

This matters now because teams are increasingly tempted to treat AI-first as a substitute for product rigor. Gilad's framing helps you stay grounded: AI is a powerful tool, but it doesn't eliminate the need to understand users, test assumptions, or iterate. If anything, it makes discovery harder because it's easier to convince yourself you don't need it.
