Teresa Torres

Nov 03
Everyone's shouting that AI changes everything. Teresa argues the opposite — and this episode is the debate she and Petra wanted to have about it.
TL;DR — PM TakeawayCore PM competencies—discovery, problem validation, measuring outcomes—don't vanish in an AI era. The tactical tools may shift, but the discipline of understanding what users actually need remains non-negotiable. Don't let AI tools seduce you into skipping the hard work of discovery.
Read more

Torres pushed back on the widespread claim that AI fundamentally rewrites product management. The episode explores where AI genuinely changes workflows (speed of iteration, new affordances, agent-based UX) and where it doesn't (the need to validate assumptions, the importance of outcomes over feature velocity, stakeholder alignment).

The conversation surfaces a real tension in tech right now: teams rushing to bolt AI onto existing products without asking whether the underlying problem-solving approach has actually improved. Torres argues that's backwards—AI should amplify good discovery practices, not replace them.

Rich Mironov

Nov 03
This is a quick win! The customer says it's trivial. Just adding a dropdown or a new data value or moving a button. Probably only 10 lines of code.
TL;DR — PM TakeawayQuick wins rarely stay quick. Mironov dissects why seemingly trivial requests balloon in scope—bad data models, missing edge cases, downstream system dependencies—and why saying yes to them trains customers and engineers to underestimate the real cost of software.
Read more

The classic trap: a customer says "just add a dropdown" and your team estimates two hours. Three weeks later it's still not done. Mironov breaks down the hidden complexity lurking inside "easy" asks—existing data structures weren't designed for the new value, there's no validation logic for the edge case, other systems depend on the old constraint, and you've now created a code path that only one customer uses.

The broader lesson: each accepted quick win trains your customer base to believe you can magically add features instantly, and it trains your engineering team to make worse architectural choices to chase velocity on paper. Mironov argues for separating true quick wins (genuinely orthogonal, no data model friction, zero downstream impact) from the ones that look quick but become anchors.

Yang shares how he used ChatGPT and custom prompts to help his mom navigate cancer treatment—organizing medical records, preparing for appointments, understanding treatment options, coordinating with family. Rather than building a flashy AI product, he identified the discrete, painful workflows that matter most when you're in crisis.

The piece illustrates product discipline in an unusual context: constraints (HIPAA concerns, the need for accuracy in medical settings), user research (talking to patients and caregivers about actual pain points), and iterative refinement based on real usage. It's a reminder that AI-native product thinking means starting with the job users need to do, not with the model's capabilities.

Swyx (Latent Space)

Nov 02
Legora can't read a single trace of what its agent does for a lawyer. The data belongs to the law firm's clients. How do you build and test an agent when you can't see its work?
TL;DR — PM TakeawayBuilding agent-based products in regulated industries forces you to rethink testing and evaluation entirely. When you can't see the agent's work, you rely on outcome metrics, structured handoffs, and trust anchors. This interview shows how constraint breeds product rigor—lessons that apply even in less regulated domains.
Read more

Jacob Lauritzen, CTO of Legora, faces a hard constraint: legal agents can't retain or expose client data, so Legora can't trace a single execution. This forces a different product model: instead of black-box debugging, the product must be built around transparency by design—showing lawyers *why* the agent made a recommendation, surfacing reasoning, building in checkpoints where humans validate before action.

The second piece, on Arca's agent for financial work, complements this by exploring how persistent memory and custom harnesses enable agents to span long workflows—days of thinking about a portfolio, maintaining context, collaborating with humans. Together, these articles show that agent-native product design isn't about unleashing AI; it's about embedding structure, guardrails, and human judgment into the workflow architecture.

The product operating model isn't just a PM reorg; it's a stakeholder realignment. Ethan Mollick

Mar 12, 2026
Worth your time regardless of when it was published
Why it's worth your timeA clear-eyed map of where AI stands and what likely comes next. Mollick identifies the jagged capability frontier—where AI is superhuman, where it's brittle, where it's still learning—and what that means for product strategy. Foundational reading for any PM building in 2026.
Read more

Mollick argues that AI's shape is fundamentally uneven. It's exceptional at pattern matching and language tasks, fragile at multi-step reasoning and novel problems, and often confidently wrong in ways that feel right. This jaggedness isn't a bug to fix; it's the constraint that shapes viable products. Teams that design *with* AI's actual shape—not the sci-fi version—ship things that work.

He also identifies bottlenecks: human expertise in the loop, annotation for training, the cost of edge-case handling. Understanding these constraints helps PMs make disciplined choices about where to deploy AI versus where to keep humans firmly in control. Essential framing for avoiding the "we'll just add AI" trap.
