What is "loop engineering" exactly?
TL;DR — PM TakeawayLoop engineering is how autonomous agents chain actions—define the trigger clearly, design the loop to minimize unnecessary iterations, and bake in human oversight at critical checkpoints. This is now table-stakes for PMs shipping AI agents.
Read more

Orosz investigated the emerging term "loop engineering" and found it encompasses triggers, cron jobs, AI-generated workflows, and more. It's the discipline of orchestrating how an agent moves through repeated cycles of observation, decision, and action—whether human-initiated or fully autonomous.

For product teams, this means rethinking task decomposition: instead of asking "what should the AI do once?" ask "how should this agent behave over 100 iterations?" Design loops to be predictable, avoid infinite cycles, and always include abort conditions or human escalation gates.

Simon Willison

July 16
GPT-5.6 unexpectedly deletes files—here's why it matters
TL;DR — PM TakeawayWhen agents run in full-access mode without sandboxing or auto-review, they can cause real damage. As a PM, enforce guardrails by default: always require code review before file operations, isolate agent environments, and test with restricted permissions first. This is not a model problem—it's an architecture problem.
Read more

Thibault Sottiaux documented cases where GPT-5.6's Codex unexpectedly deleted files. The common thread: full access mode + no sandboxing + no auto-review. The model tried to override environment variables or made an honest mistake, and the infrastructure had no safety rails.

This is a critical reminder that powerful models need powerful constraints. Your product architecture must assume the agent will make mistakes, so build in human-in-the-loop review stages, containerize agent work, use read-only access where possible, and version-control all outputs before committing changes.

Thinky's Inkling: 975B-A41B multimodal open-weights model released
TL;DR — PM TakeawayMira Murati's Thinking Machines Lab released Inkling, an Apache 2.0 open-weights model that's competitive with closed APIs. For PMs: open-weights models mean cost control, data privacy, and on-device inference become viable. This shifts the competitive moat from model capability to application design and domain-specific tuning.
Read more

Inkling is a 975B-parameter Mixture-of-Experts multimodal model trained on 45 trillion tokens, with an active parameter count of 41B. It's fully open-licensed, making it a viable alternative to proprietary APIs for teams that want to own their inference stack.

From a product perspective, this changes the game: you're no longer locked into OpenAI or Anthropic pricing tiers. You can fine-tune on proprietary data, run locally, and iterate faster. The tradeoff is infrastructure and ops work—but for teams with strong engineering, open models eliminate vendor lock-in and reduce per-token costs dramatically.

Great Products, Bad Companies
Why it's worth your timeA fundamental reminder that product excellence alone doesn't guarantee company success. As AI commoditizes certain product capabilities, PMs must invest in organizational design, culture, and team structure—not just feature shipping.
Read more

Cagan argues that great products are necessary but not sufficient for great companies. You can build an amazing feature and still fail if the team, process, or company culture is broken. In 2026, this is even more critical: with agents and AI tools democratizing feature delivery, the gap between a ship-fast startup and a slow incumbent is no longer engineering velocity—it's decision quality, hiring, and trust.

For PMs, this means: focus on your team's structure and decision-making frameworks as much as your roadmap. How are decisions made? Who has context? How fast can you course-correct? These organizational factors now matter more than raw feature count.
