Simon Willison

Jun 25
AI and Liability
TL;DR — PM TakeawayA German court ruled Google liable for errors in its AI-generated summaries. The principle: AI agents belong to the deploying organization, so your company owns the liability for inaccurate or harmful outputs. This fundamentally changes how PMs should think about QA, guardrails, and approval workflows for AI features—liability is no longer on the model vendor.
Read more

Bruce Schneier's analysis is blunt: if you hired human writers to generate summaries, your company is liable for their errors. The same logic applies to AI agents. Companies cannot use "the AI made a mistake" as a defense; they remain responsible for the outputs they publish or serve to users.

For PM teams, this means rethinking error budgets and feature rollout cadences. You need active monitoring, human-in-the-loop workflows for high-stakes outputs, and clear testing protocols before shipping. The cost of AI-generated content errors is now explicitly your cost, not the model vendor's.

For PMs building AI products, this suggests a strategy: invest in the tools and workflows that make open models usable at scale, not just wrapper products around frontier closed models. This is especially relevant if you're selling to enterprises that care about control and cost predictability.

Slow down to speed up: so much has changed in 6 months' time
TL;DR — PM TakeawayThe engineering landscape has shifted radically in six months—AI coding tools, agent frameworks, and team structures are all in flux. Orosz argues that slowing down, consolidating, and letting teams adapt is smarter than chasing every new capability. For PMs, this is a reminder that velocity without stability kills learning; use this window to build practices and culture that stick, not just ship fast.
Read more

Orosz catalogs the changes: AI code generation shifted from "helper" to "agent," teams are experimenting with flat structures to avoid AI-era silos, and the cost-benefit of certain technologies has inverted. Companies that tried to adopt every new framework burned out their teams; those that paused and selected carefully are moving faster now with less churn.

The PM implication: your roadmap should include "consolidation sprints" and explicit time to let teams stabilize. It sounds counterintuitive, but building in slack now—time for learning, tool standardization, and culture-setting—reduces rework later.
