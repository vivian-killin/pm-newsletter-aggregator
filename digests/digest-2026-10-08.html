Torres and Wille examine the shift toward recording every meeting and conversation through AI note-takers and wearable devices. This move toward perfect recall challenges traditional team dynamics where some things were intentionally forgotten, allowing people to move past mistakes and speak freely.

The episode surfaces a real tension for product builders: when every word is captured and fact-checkable, does it change how people communicate? Do teams become more defensive? Do people self-censor? These dynamics matter for PMs designing tools that sit in the middle of team workflows and information flow.

Julie Zhuo

Oct 7
AI, mental gyms, and the most important choice we face
TL;DR — PM TakeawayZhuo argues that as AI handles more cognitive work, the critical question shifts from "what can AI do" to "what do we want humans to do with their minds." PMs should use this lens when deciding which tasks to automate—not every efficiency gain is worth the cost to human judgment and growth.
Read more

Zhuo draws a parallel between the decline of physical skills (citing Wild Bill Hickok's era of exceptional physical prowess versus today's baseline fitness) and the potential decline of cognitive skills. She argues that AI doesn't just automate work—it shapes what humans practice and develop.

For product builders, this reframes the automation question. It's not enough to ask "can AI do this faster?" You need to ask "if we remove this task from humans, what skill atrophies, and is that a net win?" This is especially acute for products aimed at knowledge workers, where the boundary between helpful automation and skill erosion is blurry.

Itamar Gilad

Jun 3
Worth your time regardless of when it was published
Why it's worth your timeGilad argues that the "skateboard to car" MVP framework misses how discovery actually works in code-first teams. For PMs in AI-native orgs, this reframes what "discovery" looks like when engineers ship fast and iterate—and where PMs should push back or lean in.
Read more

Gilad challenges the conventional wisdom about building simple prototypes first. He argues that in certain contexts—especially when tools are code-first—the minimal version that teaches you something isn't the simplest UI; it's the working system. This is deeply relevant to AI product teams, where "minimal" often means "AI model + basic interface," not "paper prototype."

The takeaway for PMs: rethink what discovery means in a code-first, AI-native setting. Sometimes you discover by shipping an AI feature to a small cohort, not by running user interviews about a mockup. This shifts your role toward rapid iteration, hypothesis setting, and learning velocity rather than upfront specification.
